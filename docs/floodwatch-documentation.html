How FloodWatch sees floods

A river-flow forecast turned into a warning level for each municipality, shown next to the readings that the water agencies measure themselves. Built as the sibling of FireWatch, but a different kind of system.

01 Forecast, not detection

FireWatch can say a fire is burning now, because satellites report it within minutes. Nothing equivalent exists for floods, and that decides the whole design.

A flood has no thermal signature to detect. Sentinel-1 radar can see standing water through cloud, but it revisits only every few days. So the core product here is a forecast: how much water each river is expected to carry over the next seven days, compared with how often that much water has happened before. Measured gauge readings from the agencies are shown beside it, but they are a separate layer and are never blended into the forecast level.

What a level means

A level says that the forecast flow in a ~5 km model cell reaches a value that occurs about once in 2, 5 or 20 years. It does not say water is in anyone's street, and it is not an official warning. The gauges are the measured side of the picture.

03 From flow to warning level

Open-Meteo serves a flow in m³/s and nothing else: no idea what is "high" for a given river. Levels are therefore derived from the cell's own history.

  1. Take each year's single highest daily flow (33 years, 1993 to the last full year).
  2. Fit a Gumbel (extreme-value) distribution to those yearly maxima by the method of moments.
  3. Read off the flows expected once in 2, 5 and 20 years: these are watch, warning and severe.

Only today and later days can raise a level, because a flood that has already crested is history, not news. The window is judged by its highest day, since a mean would smooth a two-day crest below its threshold. A municipality is as bad as its worst river.

Estimates, not official figures

With 33 yearly maxima the 20-year figure is an extrapolation. Treat levels as relative severity. They have not been compared with any official threshold.

04 Choosing river points

The model is a grid, not a river network, so something has to decide which cell is which river. scripts/build_points.py does that once and commits the result.

  1. Take the 30-day mean flow of every grid cell inside any municipality (2,312 cells).
  2. For each municipality keep its strongest cells, and also a few strong cells just outside it (within 8 km).
  3. Name each cell by its nearest named OpenStreetMap river. A cell outside the municipality counts only if that river has a stretch inside it.
  4. Keep the best cell per river, at most five rivers per municipality.
  5. Fetch 33 years of history for the chosen cells and derive the thresholds.

Why more than one river, and why cells outside

The Bosna and the Krivaja flood together at Zavidovići, so one point per municipality could only ever show one of them. And a cell is 5 km wide: a river that runs along a border or through a small municipality can have its cell centre just outside it. Counting only cells inside left 42 municipalities with no river at all; allowing nearby cells brought that to 25. Raising the cap from three rivers to five then recovered strong rivers that were being dropped (Tavna at Bijeljina, Krušnica at Bosanska Krupa, Vrbanja at Banja Luka). 232 river points in 120 municipalities.

Which river is a heuristic

The model gives no river identity. "Nearest named river within 6 km" is a judgement, and a cell near a confluence can be named for the wrong river. 99 of the 207 points use a nearby cell, and the popup says so. Review the picks after every rebuild.

25 municipalities still have no river point, among them Centar and the other Sarajevo municipalities, Travnik, Gradačac and Srebrenik. The likely reason is that a 5 km model does not resolve those small rivers; that has not been confirmed municipality by municipality. The map says "no significant river in the model", which is accurate, and does not say those places have no river.

05 Measured gauges

Three agencies publish live gauge readings. They are drawn as squares, kept visually apart from the forecast circles, and follow strict rules:

  • Values appear exactly as published. Nothing is rescaled, smoothed or merged.
  • The only class shown is one the source publishes itself: the Sava agency's N, P, R, V, Z. Nothing is derived from a reading.
  • Each source is isolated. One failing never costs the others.
  • Nothing is wired to alerts or to any public output until written permission is on file.

History is what we recorded

The Sava agency publishes history only as images and spreadsheets, and fetching those for 99 stations every hour would be an unreasonable load on a public server. So each poll saves every new reading (deduplicated on the source's own timestamp, up to 96 per station) and the popup draws that record as a small line. A new install shows no line until a station has two readings.

06 The map

The layout deliberately follows FireWatch's, so the two read as one family: a docked side panel, map tools on the left, a language switch on top, a collapsible legend and a bottom bar. On a phone the panel becomes a drawer with an alert badge.

  • Header with a status dot and a count such as "4 općine pod upozorenjem" (correct Bosnian plural forms), the update time, and a warning if the data is over 9 hours old.
  • Day chips where FireWatch has time ranges: today, tomorrow, two more days, and "7 days" (the worst level over the whole window). Each shows how many municipalities are on alert.
  • Cards for municipalities on alert, with the river, the flow, the peak, a bar showing the flow as a share of the watch level, and a small forecast chart. When there is no alert, the panel lists the rivers closest to the watch level instead.
  • Bottom bar with one button per forecast day, coloured by the worst level that day, plus play and speed controls that step through the days.
  • Shading and river stretches by worst river level, in blue, violet and magenta, deliberately unlike FireWatch's orange and red. The stretch is drawn only inside its municipality, and thick when flagged.
  • Layers (rivers, municipalities, gauges when present), an eye button that hides them all, and a legend.
  • Bosnian by default, English on a toggle, remembered per device.
What the map does not show

Only rivers the forecast model can resolve (~5 km) are drawn. Of 394 pairs of a municipality and a named OpenStreetMap river, the map covered about half before the cap was raised; around 36% have no model flow at all (under 1 m³/s), so they cannot be forecast whatever we do. The map says this in its legend, in every popup and in the empty state, because a calm map is easily read as "my small river is fine".

Level per day is computed in the browser from each river's daily flows and its thresholds, so the chips, the bottom bar and the 7-day view always agree.

Open the map over http, not from the file

OpenStreetMap's tile servers answer a request with no Referer by sending back a "403 Access blocked" image with HTTP 200, so nothing looks like an error. A page opened from file:// sends no Referer. python3 -m floodwatch map therefore serves the page on http://127.0.0.1:8765/, and that is what to open. The tile policy requires a valid Referer from web pages and a published page must not set a restrictive Referrer-Policy.

07 Rate limits & cost

Open-Meteo's free tier allows 10,000 calls a day, 5,000 an hour and 600 a minute, applied per IP address, for non-commercial use. Calls are weighted by locations multiplied by days, which is what makes the first build expensive.

Measured behaviour of the flood API
RequestResult
50 locations × 30 daysFine; used for the first selection
50 locations × 33 yearsHTTP 400 "requests too much data"; the archive uses 3 per call
93 days × 2,312 cellsExhausted the hourly limit; repeated tries then exhausted the daily one
Poll: ~150 cells × 10 daysLight; one cycle succeeded straight after the build

There are three distinct 429 messages: Minutely, Hourly and Daily … try again tomorrow. Only the first is worth waiting out, so the client waits a minute for it and raises on the other two. The build is resumable: every fetched cell is cached, so a run that dies resumes where it stopped.

08 Field notes

Behaviour that is in nobody's documentation, found by measuring.

The grid snaps, and a cell centre is not the river

The API snaps any coordinate to a 0.05° grid with centres at 0.025 + k×0.05. Asking for centres gives one cell reproducibly. A centre can sit up to ~3.5 km from the water, so markers are moved onto a real river line for display.

Non-river cells answer zero, not an error

A hillside cell returns 0.0. A threshold of 1 m³/s separates those from rivers.

One point per municipality was a design bug

It was found by asking about Zavidovići, where two rivers flood together. The fix needed nearby cells too, and separate pools for inside and nearby cells: in the first attempt, cells beside the large Bosna crowded out the municipality's own tributaries.

Tiles can fail while returning 200

See section 6. It is the same trap as FireWatch's EUMETView and FIRMS answers: a 200 that carries nothing useful.

Headless Chrome does not reproduce the tile block

Screenshots from file:// showed real tiles even though the user's browser showed blocked ones. A screenshot therefore cannot verify the Referer fix; the curl test and the user's own browser did.

Not every popup fits the screen

A popup with three rivers ran off the top of the window with auto-pan disabled. Popups now have a maximum height and scroll.

The browser may not know Bosnian month names

A headless Chrome printed "M10 24" for a Bosnian date, so month and weekday names are written out in the page, as FireWatch does.

A fresh clone has no state folder

The first deploy failed because state/ holds only gitignored output and git keeps no empty folders. Every writer now creates it first.

The RHMZ feed has no unit

Neither the feed nor the page states one, so none is shown. Guessing "cm" would have put a number with a unit it may not have.

10 Operations

python3 scripts/build_boundaries.py   light polygons for the map
python3 scripts/build_rivers.py        OSM river lines (Overpass, with mirrors)
python3 scripts/build_points.py        river points + thresholds (resumable)
python3 -m floodwatch poll             one cycle: fetch, assess, write the snapshot
python3 -m floodwatch map [port]       serve the map on http://127.0.0.1:8765/
python3 tests_levels.py                11 assertions; exits non-zero on failure

There is no package manager and no build step; the only dependency is requests. The map's JavaScript has no automated tests, by the same decision as FireWatch: verify map changes by rendering and looking. If a build dies on a rate limit, just run it again.

11 Limits & roadmap

  • It is a forecast of a model. A ~5 km grid cannot see small rivers or flash floods in narrow valleys, and no alert never means no risk.
  • No real warning has been seen yet. On the first real poll every river was far below its watch flow (at most 32%), so the coloured states are untested on real data.
  • Gauges do not raise a municipality's level. That would blend a measurement into a forecast, and waits on permissions.
  • Not published. No repository, workflow or site exists yet, and nothing sends alerts.

Next

  1. Publish a map-only version (forecast, no gauges, no alerts) on GitHub Pages, with a poll workflow like FireWatch's. Open-Meteo applies limits per IP and warns that shared platforms may be limited sooner, so a hosted poll may sometimes be refused.
  2. Send the three permission emails; enable the gauge layer publicly only after written replies.
  3. Add Telegram alerts: a new bot and channels, a store so an alert is sent once, and nothing triggered by testing.
